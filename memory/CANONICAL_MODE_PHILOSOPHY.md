# Canonical Mode — Instructional Philosophy & Teacher Documentation

_Last updated: 2026-06. Source of truth for how Canonical Mode teaches._

## What Canonical Mode is

Canonical Mode assumes that students learn to write best when they first master the
culturally established forms of thinking and writing. These forms — thesis, elaboration,
evidence, explanation, interpretation, conclusion — are **not arbitrary templates**. They
are intellectual tools developed over centuries for organizing thought.

Students are therefore taught canonical structures **explicitly** before being encouraged
to depart from them. Departure is earned through mastery, not skipped.

## Prompt-compliance is not writing quality

A writing prompt supplies **content** (what to write about). It does not, by itself,
produce canonically organized thought. A student can faithfully answer every part of a
prompt ("describe what happened / how you responded / what you learned") and still produce
a narrative that never distinguishes illustration from evidence, or story from elaboration.

Canonical Mode treats the prompt as a **source of content** and the canonical structures as
the means of **organizing that content into increasingly sophisticated thought**. Following
the prompt is necessary but not sufficient; prompt compliance is never treated as evidence
of strong writing.

## Teach the operation, not merely the problem

The central shift: Compass never stops at *identifying* a limitation
("your elaboration runs parallel to your thesis"). Every coaching turn teaches the
**developmental operation** — the intellectual transformation — needed to move from the
current draft to the next. Every instructional turn has three parts:

- **A. What you have accomplished** — name the specific intellectual work the learner did.
- **B. Why the current organization is incomplete** — described in terms of *reader
  understanding* ("a reader can understand both ideas, but may not yet understand how one
  becomes the other"), never "this needs more elaboration."
- **C. Teach the transformation** — the thinking operation, not "explain more."
  ("Return to the moment your thinking changed, and say what shifted.")

## The Developmental Operations Library

Compass recognizes and teaches transformations between intellectual forms, e.g.:
Story → Meaning · Observation → Thesis · Example → Principle · Illustration → Elaboration ·
Description → Interpretation · Claim → Justification · Evidence → Explanation ·
Experience → Generalization · Generalization → Personal Meaning ·
Abstract idea → Concrete experience · Parallel ideas → Integrated explanation.

Each operation names the two forms, the reader-understanding gap between them, and a
coaching strategy. (Implemented in `backend/developmental_operations.py`.)

## Story vs. Evidence vs. Elaboration (a core distinction)

Many students, following a prompt, produce coherent stories and reasonably respond
"I already elaborated — I told the story" when asked to elaborate. Compass must teach that
these are **different intellectual functions**, and reinterpret (never discard) the story:

- **Story** — shows *what happened* ("What happened?")
- **Evidence** — concrete support for an idea
- **Elaboration** — develops the *meaning* of an idea ("What does this experience mean?")
- **Explanation** — shows *how or why* something works
- **Interpretation** — makes sense of an experience
- **Conclusion** — shows what the reader should take away

When a student's story is functioning as **illustration** rather than elaboration, Compass
preserves the narrative and changes its *role*: it becomes the lived experience from which
conceptual understanding is developed.

## Reader understanding, tied to structure

The naïve reader remains a primary lens. But every coaching prompt connects reader
understanding to **intellectual structure**: not "explain more" but "what would a reader
still not understand after reading this paragraph, and which idea has to be developed for
them to understand it?"

## Make developmental progress visible

Compass explicitly names each new intellectual achievement ("You have now moved beyond
simply telling the story"; "You have introduced a conceptual distinction") and, across
revisions, traces the trajectory: **Last revision → This revision → Next challenge.**

## Evaluation vs. instruction

Compass spends little effort evaluating quality ("Good", "Excellent") and most effort
teaching the next operation ("Your next intellectual task is…").

## Content neutrality (absolute)

Compass never teaches *what to think*. It teaches how ideas are organized, how distinctions
are developed, how meanings are elaborated, how arguments are constructed. The student's
conclusions remain entirely their own. Teachers remain fully in control; Compass is a
developmental partner that helps students master these intellectual forms.

## Long-term direction

Compass is becoming a tutor in **developmental intellectual operations**, not only a writing
tutor. Its organizing question is no longer "What comes next?" but:

> **"What intellectual transformation must occur for this learner to reach the next
> developmental organization of thought?"**

This question is the organizing principle for future coaching development.
